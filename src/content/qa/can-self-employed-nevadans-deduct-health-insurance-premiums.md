This is the most widely available tax advantage in self-employed health coverage, and one of the most commonly fumbled. Not because the concept is hard. Because the limits sit in places nobody looks until April.

## Who qualifies for the self-employed health insurance deduction?

Self-employed filers with net earnings from the business: sole proprietors, partners, and more-than-2-percent S corporation shareholders under a specific payroll sequence. Coverage has to be established under the business.

The deduction lives on Schedule 1 of Form 1040, line 17. That placement matters more than it sounds, because it reduces adjusted gross income before the standard deduction is applied. A Las Vegas freelancer who takes the standard deduction still gets the full benefit here. Nothing has to be itemized.

S corporation owners follow a different path to the same destination. Because a shareholder owning more than 2 percent is treated like a partner rather than an employee for fringe benefit purposes, premiums generally have to be paid or reimbursed by the corporation and reported on the shareholder's W-2 before the personal deduction can be claimed. The IRS lays out that sequence in its guidance on [S corporation compensation and medical insurance issues](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/s-corporation-compensation-and-medical-insurance-issues). Skip the payroll reporting step and the deduction is not available, no matter how much was actually spent on premiums. The related structural question is covered in [can an S corp owner use an ICHRA](/qa/can-an-s-corp-owner-use-an-ichra).

## Which premiums actually count?

Medical, dental, vision and qualified long-term care premiums, paid for the taxpayer, a spouse, dependents, and a child who is under age 27 at the end of the tax year.

That last category surprises people. The child does not have to be a dependent. A 25-year-old covered on a parent's plan while working part time in Henderson still counts for this purpose, which is a genuinely useful detail for households carrying adult children.

Dental and vision belong in the calculation too, and they get left out routinely because the premiums are small and billed separately. Small premiums still deduct.

## What limits the deduction?

Two rules, and both are quiet enough to miss. The amount cannot exceed net self-employment income, and any month of eligibility for a subsidized employer plan is disqualified.

### The net income cap

The deduction is limited by net self-employment income from the business under which the plan is established. A rough year caps the deduction regardless of what was paid in premiums.

This bites hardest in exactly the businesses that fill this valley. Commission income, seasonal contracting, event work tied to the convention calendar, a rideshare year interrupted by a slow summer. Premiums are paid twelve months a year. Profit is not earned evenly across those twelve months.

Premiums that cannot be deducted on line 17 are not automatically lost. They may be included as medical expenses on Schedule A if the household itemizes, subject to the usual medical expense threshold.

### The employer-eligibility rule

The deduction is unavailable for any month the taxpayer was eligible to participate in a subsidized health plan maintained by the taxpayer's own employer, a spouse's employer, or the employer of a dependent or a child under 27.

Eligibility is the trigger. Not enrollment. A self-employed Nevadan who declines a spouse's employer plan and keeps a marketplace policy has still lost the deduction for those months. The interaction gets worked through in [can a spouse join a self-employed health plan](/qa/can-a-spouse-join-a-self-employed-health-plan).

One structure to watch: coverage under a QSEHRA counts as participation in a subsidized health plan, which blocks this deduction outright. Anyone weighing a reimbursement arrangement should read [tax-advantaged health benefits for the self-employed](/blog-post/tax-advantaged-health-benefits-self-employed) before setting one up, because trading a deduction for an arrangement is a real possibility and rarely an intentional one.

## When is Form 7206 required?

Three situations trigger it: more than one source of self-employment income, a return that includes Form 2555, and any claim that uses qualified long-term care amounts.

Plenty of self-employed Nevadans hit the first one without realizing. A Realtor with a second consulting LLC, a bartender running a side photography business, a contractor with an equipment rental sideline. Two Schedule C businesses is two sources. The [Form 7206 instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i7206) walk through the computation, and the form exists precisely because those situations break the simple version.

## How does the deduction interact with premium tax credits?

Circularly, and that is not a figure of speech. Deducting premiums lowers income, lower income raises the credit, a larger credit shrinks the premium actually paid, and a smaller paid premium shrinks the deduction.

The IRS publishes methods for resolving the loop. Running them is routine work for a tax professional and error-prone for everyone else. What matters on the coverage side is that the income estimate given to Nevada Health Link drives the advance credit, and that estimate gets reconciled at filing. Estimating badly is the single most common failure among 1099 households here, which is why [how freelancers estimate income for health subsidies](/qa/how-do-freelancers-estimate-income-for-health-subsidies) exists as its own page.

Worth knowing separately: this deduction reduces income tax, but it is not a business expense that lowers self-employment tax. The IRS covers what [self-employment tax](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employment-tax-social-security-and-medicare-taxes) is calculated on, and health premiums are not in that calculation.

## What does Nevada change about the answer?

Nevada has no state income tax, so the federal deduction is the entire benefit. Nothing stacks at the state level, and nothing softens a federal mistake either.

Filers moving here from California or Oregon often expect a second layer of relief and plan around one that does not exist. The upside is simplicity. The downside is that a missed month, a botched S corporation payroll sequence, or an unnoticed spouse offer costs the full amount rather than part of it.

Keep premium statements, note the exact months of any employer offer in the household, and track dental and vision separately from major medical. Those three habits recover more money than most tax strategies do.
