Ask a room full of Strip cocktail servers what they earn and the honest answer is two numbers: the wage the casino pays and the tips the guests pay. Health insurance subsidies care about both, and the second one is where estimates go wrong.

## Do reported tips count toward marketplace subsidy income?

Yes. Reported tips are taxable income, and the marketplace counts them in the modified adjusted gross income figure that sets subsidy amounts. Base wages alone are not the income number; wages plus reported tips plus everything else taxable is the income number.

Modified adjusted gross income, usually shortened to MAGI, starts from the adjusted gross income on a federal tax return and adds back a few specific items. For a tipped worker the practical version is simple: whatever lands on the W-2, which includes reported tips, plus any other household income such as a spouse's pay, side work, or unemployment benefits. The federal marketplace publishes a usable list of [what counts as income](https://www.healthcare.gov/income-and-household-information/income/), and it reads the way a tax return reads, not the way a pay stub reads.

The stakes are specific to this town. In much of the country, tips are a rounding error on total pay. In Las Vegas hospitality, tips are routinely the majority of take-home for servers, bartenders, dealers, valets, and bell staff. An income estimate that ignores them is not slightly low. It can be half the real number.

## How does tip reporting connect to the subsidy calculation?

Through the W-2 and the tax return. Tips reported to the employer flow onto the W-2 with wages, the tax return turns that into adjusted gross income, and the marketplace measures the subsidy against that figure, not against the hourly rate.

Employees are required to report cash tips to their employer, and card tips are captured automatically in most modern hospitality payroll. The IRS spells out the obligations in its guidance on [tip recordkeeping and reporting](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/tip-recordkeeping-and-reporting). Between card-heavy payment habits and casino toke committees that formalize tip pooling and reporting, most Strip workers' reported tip income is well documented, which means the marketplace and the IRS are looking at the same number.

That documentation is exactly why lowballing an estimate does not work as a strategy. Subsidies are advanced monthly against the estimate, then reconciled on the federal return against the real number. When the W-2 shows more income than the application projected, the extra subsidy that was advanced generally has to be paid back, subject to caps at some income levels. The repayment mechanics are covered in [whether ACA subsidies have to be paid back](/qa/do-aca-subsidies-have-to-be-paid-back), and the broader definition question in [what income counts for ACA subsidies](/qa/what-income-counts-for-aca-subsidies).

## What about the recent federal tax changes on tips?

Federal law on how tip income is taxed has changed in recent years, and headlines about tips and taxes have left many workers assuming tips no longer count for anything. That assumption is not safe to make for subsidy purposes.

The income definition for marketplace subsidies is set by its own rules, and whether any tip-related deduction reduces the specific income figure used for subsidies is a technical question about where that deduction sits on the return. The structural point workers should carry: reporting obligations for tips have not disappeared, tips remain income, and no one should drop tips from a marketplace estimate based on a headline. We are insurance nerds, not tax professionals, and how current tip rules land on a specific return is a question for a licensed tax professional at filing time.

What a broker can responsibly say is this: build the estimate from documented totals, keep it current, and let the tax return, prepared by someone qualified to prepare it, settle the final numbers.

## How should a tipped worker actually build the estimate?

Start from the most recent W-2, because it already contains reported tips, then adjust for what this year will genuinely do differently: more hours or fewer, a station change, a property change, a slow convention calendar.

A few practices keep the estimate honest through a hospitality year.

**Use twelve months, not a season.** Pool season on a dayclub, December on the Strip, and a dead August produce wildly different weekly numbers. Annualizing a hot stretch overstates income; annualizing a dead one understates it. The W-2 smooths all of it.

**Count the whole household.** A server married to a union cook, or to a rideshare driver, estimates household income, not personal income. Both incomes move the subsidy.

**Update mid-year, every time something shifts.** Picking up a second gig, dropping to part time, a layoff, a big station upgrade: each is worth a five-minute income update with Nevada Health Link so the subsidy adjusts monthly instead of correcting all at once in April. A schedule collapse can also change the answer entirely, which is covered in [what happens to health insurance during a casino layoff](/qa/what-happens-to-health-insurance-during-a-casino-layoff).

**Do not fear the honest number.** Some workers understate income deliberately, thinking a bigger subsidy now is free money. Reconciliation makes it a loan. Others overstate to be safe and quietly overpay premiums all year; filing recovers it, but the money was theirs all along.
