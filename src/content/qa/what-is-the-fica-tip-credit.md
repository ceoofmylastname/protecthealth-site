This is the generous corner of tipped payroll. A federal credit that hands real money back to qualifying food and beverage employers, and one that goes unclaimed in Las Vegas constantly.

## What is the FICA tip credit and how does the credit work?

The FICA tip credit is a federal business tax credit for the employer share of Social Security and Medicare taxes paid on certain employee tips. The credit is claimed on the business return rather than inside the payroll system.

The chain of events starts with reporting. Tips are wages for federal tax purposes, so employees report tips to the employer, the employer withholds income tax and the employee share of Social Security and Medicare on those reported tips, and the employer then pays its own share of Social Security and Medicare on the same amounts. The full sequence is laid out in the IRS guidance on [tip recordkeeping and reporting](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/tip-recordkeeping-and-reporting).

That last step is the expense the credit addresses. Employer-side payroll tax on tip income is a genuine cost of running a tipped business, and federal law returns a portion of it to qualifying employers.

One boundary is fixed in statute and worth memorizing. The credit cannot be claimed for tips used to meet a minimum wage of $7.25 per hour, the federal rate in effect on July 24, 2009. That figure does not move when other wage rates move.

## Which businesses qualify for the credit?

Food and beverage establishments where tipping is customary. Restaurants, bars, catering operations and similar venues sit squarely inside the category the credit was written for.

The IRS describes the rules on its [FICA tip credit for employers](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/fica-tip-credit-for-employers) page, and the filing itself happens on [Form 8846, Credit for Employer Social Security and Medicare Taxes Paid on Certain Employee Tips](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8846).

Las Vegas is unusually dense with qualifying operations. Neighborhood restaurants off Eastern and Sahara, cocktail bars in the Arts District, brewery taprooms in Henderson, caterers working the convention corridor, and pool and cabana food service across the valley all run on tipped food and beverage labor. Volume per employee here runs high compared with most of the country, which raises both the payroll tax paid and the credit potentially available.

### The Nevada wrinkle that costs owners money

Nevada prohibits a tip credit against the minimum wage. A surprising number of owners hear that sentence, conclude that no tip-related credit exists in this state, and never ask another question.

That conclusion is wrong, and it is expensive at Las Vegas tip volumes. Two different rules are in play. The Nevada rule governs wages owed to an employee, and the detail lives in [does Nevada allow a tip credit](/qa/does-nevada-allow-a-tip-credit). The federal FICA tip credit governs employer payroll tax on the business return. State wage law does not disqualify a Nevada employer from a federal payroll tax credit.

There is an argument that the credit reaches further here precisely because of the state rule. Nevada requires the full applicable minimum wage in direct cash wages, so tips are not being consumed to satisfy a wage floor in the way the federal calculation contemplates.

## What does the credit look like in practice?

The size of the credit tracks reported tip volume, so busy tipped rooms with clean reporting see the largest numbers. A single location with a full front of house can produce a meaningful figure.

### An illustrative example

The figures below are invented purely to show the shape of the calculation. These are not a quote, not a projection, and not a substitute for an actual return.

Picture a neighborhood Italian restaurant off Eastern Avenue with nine tipped servers, open six days a week, steady local business with a lift during heavy convention weeks. Suppose reported tips across those nine servers total roughly $13,500 in a month, or about $162,000 across the year. The employer share of Social Security and Medicare runs 7.65 percent, which on that tip volume works out to roughly $12,400 of employer payroll tax attributable to tips.

For a restaurant of that size, a credit computed off a base like that is not a rounding error. It is the kind of number that changes whether a second walk-in cooler gets replaced this year.

Real outcomes differ from the illustration in every direction. Wage bases, the fixed $7.25 threshold, interactions with other credits, and how the deduction interacts with the credit all change the answer. That is exactly why the calculation belongs with a licensed tax professional.

## Why does the FICA tip credit go unclaimed so often?

Because the credit lives on the income tax return while the underlying data lives in the payroll system, and in most small businesses those two things are handled by different people who never compare notes.

A restaurant owner sees payroll every two weeks and taxes once a year. The bookkeeper processing payroll is not preparing the return. The preparer working from a summarized profit and loss statement may never see tip detail at all unless somebody surfaces it. Nobody is being careless. The information simply never lands in the same room.

Two other conditions have to hold before the credit can be claimed cleanly, and both fail regularly.

**Tip reporting has to be systematic.** A reporting process that lives in a manager's memory of what a server said at close of shift is not a record. A defined method, a defined deadline, and a retained record are the minimum. Salons, valet operations and independent contractors working inside a larger venue are where this most often falls apart.

**Service charges have to be separated from tips.** Mandatory banquet fees and auto-gratuities are wages, not tips, and they carry no credit eligibility. A venue coding them through the tip line is simultaneously overstating a credit and understating overtime, which is covered in [are service charges taxed like tips](/qa/are-service-charges-taxed-like-tips). Pool composition matters for the same reason, and that is worked through in [who can legally share a tip pool](/qa/who-can-legally-share-a-tip-pool).
