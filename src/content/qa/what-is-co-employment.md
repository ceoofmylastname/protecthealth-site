Co-employment is the legal engine inside every PEO. It is also the word that makes owners nervous until somebody shows them what actually gets divided.

## What is co-employment and what does the arrangement divide?

Co-employment is a contractual structure that splits the employer role into an administrative half and an operational half. The PEO takes the administrative half. The business keeps everything to do with running the business.

| Shifts to the PEO | Stays with the business |
|---|---|
| Payroll processing and tax filings | Hiring and firing decisions |
| Benefits sponsorship and administration | Wages, roles and promotions |
| Workers compensation coverage and claims | Scheduling and daily direction of work |
| Payroll-related compliance documentation | Culture, customers and strategy |

A line cook in a Las Vegas kitchen notices one thing after a transition: a different name at the top of the pay stub. Same chef, same station, same schedule, same reason for showing up. What a PEO handles day to day is broken out in [what does a PEO do](/qa/what-does-a-peo-do).

## Why does co-employment exist at all?

Because scale buys pricing that a small employer cannot buy alone, and pooling requires a shared employer relationship to work. The structure is a means to an economic end.

Consider a twenty-two person specialty contractor in North Las Vegas. Standing alone, that business negotiates benefits and workers compensation as a twenty-two person risk. Inside a co-employment pool covering tens of thousands of worksite employees, the same business is a fraction of an enormous group, and pricing follows group size rather than individual headcount.

That pooling is the entire economic case behind the fee, which is why the cost analysis in [how much does a PEO cost](/qa/how-much-does-a-peo-cost) is really a purchasing power analysis. The fee buys admission.

There is a second and less advertised benefit. Administrative consolidation means one relationship instead of a payroll vendor, a benefits broker, a workers compensation carrier relationship and an HR consultant, each with its own portal and its own renewal date. For an owner already working sixty hours, the value of consolidation is measured in evenings.

### What co-employment is definitely not

Not staffing. Not leasing. Not surrendering the team to a third party.

A staffing agency supplies people who work for the agency. Under co-employment, existing employees remain the business's employees in every operational sense, and no third party recruits, directs or removes them. The distinction matters legally and it matters emotionally, because the phrase co-employment sounds like something is being handed over that is not.

Nor does the arrangement dissolve every obligation. Worksite safety is still the employer's job. Lawful daily treatment of employees is still the employer's job. The accuracy of the hours, classifications and pay rates fed into payroll is still the employer's job, and a PEO cannot correct data it never receives.

## How does co-employment affect health coverage and benefits?

Employees typically gain access to benefits sponsored through the PEO's pooled arrangement, which is frequently the headline reason a small business explores co-employment in the first place. Underlying federal obligations do not transfer with the paperwork.

Applicable large employer status generally begins at 50 or more full-time employees including equivalents, and the Internal Revenue Service publishes the [employer provisions under the Affordable Care Act](https://www.irs.gov/affordable-care-act/employers) that define the test. That determination follows the business, not the vendor. An owner near that threshold should know the number before signing anything, not after.

Benefits sitting inside a co-employment arrangement also behave differently at renewal than a standalone group plan does. Renewal happens inside the pool, which smooths some volatility and removes some control. Owners comparing that behavior against a conventional small group plan will find the landscape mapped in [the small business health insurance guide](/blog-post/small-business-health-insurance).

Anyone in Nevada advising on the insurance components should be licensed here. The [Nevada Division of Insurance](https://doi.nv.gov/) licenses producers in this state, and license status is verifiable in a couple of minutes. That check is worth running on any party recommending a benefits arrangement, whatever the packaging.

We are insurance nerds, not tax professionals. Payroll tax mechanics inside a co-employment relationship, including how filings are handled under a shared employer identification, are questions for a licensed tax professional.

## What should a Las Vegas owner understand before entering co-employment?

Three things, and all three are contract questions rather than sales questions. Each one is easier to answer before signing than after.

**Where the line sits in writing.** A good agreement states plainly which duties belong to which party. Vague division is where disputes start, particularly around employee discipline, documentation and workers compensation claims.

**What happens to benefits at exit.** Coverage sponsored through the arrangement ends with the arrangement, which makes the departure plan part of the entry decision. The logistics are covered in [can a business leave a PEO](/qa/can-a-business-leave-a-peo).

**Whether the fee structure survives growth.** Percentage-of-payroll pricing scales with the business, so a company planning to double headcount during a strong convention year should model the fee at the larger number rather than today's.

Seasonality makes that last point sharper in this valley than in most markets. A catering operation, event staffing company or hospitality vendor whose headcount swings with the convention calendar has a payroll base that moves several times a year, and percentage-based pricing moves with it in both directions.
