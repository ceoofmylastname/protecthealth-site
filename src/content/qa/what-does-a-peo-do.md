PEO is the acronym owners start hearing right about the time the back office begins to break. Here is what the arrangement actually is, minus the sales gloss.

## What does a PEO do for a small business?

A PEO becomes the co-employer of the workforce, taking on payroll processing and tax filings, benefits administration, workers compensation and HR compliance support under one contract. Operational control stays with the owner.

The split is cleaner than most owners expect before seeing it written down.

| The PEO carries | The owner keeps |
|---|---|
| Payroll and tax filings under its employer identification | Hiring and firing decisions |
| Benefits sponsorship and administration | Wages, roles and promotions |
| Workers compensation coverage and claims handling | Scheduling and daily management |
| HR compliance support and documentation | Culture, customers and strategy |

From the floor, almost nothing looks different. Same manager, same schedule, same building. Paychecks and tax documents simply arrive under a different employer identification. The legal structure making that possible is explained in [what is co-employment](/qa/what-is-co-employment).

## Why would a Las Vegas business hand off employer administration?

For two reasons that show up in different order depending on the business. Purchasing power on benefits, and the recovery of hours the owner is currently spending on paperwork.

Take purchasing power first. A PEO covering thousands of small employers buys benefits and workers compensation as one enormous group, so an eighteen-person company inside that pool can price closer to a large employer than to a small one. For a Henderson contractor competing with regional firms for licensed tradespeople, benefits pricing is a hiring weapon rather than a line item.

The second reason is quieter and usually more urgent. Somewhere between the tenth and twenty-fifth employee, employer administration stops being a Sunday chore and becomes a part-time job nobody was hired to do. New hire paperwork, terminations, wage questions, workers compensation claims, benefits enrollment, and the annual scramble to reconcile everything. The warning signs are catalogued in [the HR warning signs checklist](/blog-post/signs-your-business-needs-hr-support), which also covers which pieces can be handed off individually rather than all at once.

### The Las Vegas version of the problem

This valley has a particular shape to the administrative load. Hospitality and food service run high turnover, which means constant onboarding and offboarding. Trades and construction carry workers compensation classifications that reward getting the paperwork right. Convention season swings headcount hard, and a business that adds twenty temporary event staff in March is doing twenty rounds of employer administration in a compressed window.

Medical and dental practices in Summerlin and the southwest face a different pressure, competing for clinical staff against hospital systems that offer full benefits departments. Property management firms handle scattered worksites with a payroll administrator who is also the office manager and also the person answering the phone.

None of those businesses is failing. All of them are spending owner attention on administration rather than on revenue.

## What does a PEO not do?

A PEO does not run the business, does not supply workers, and does not absorb every legal obligation attached to being an employer. Mistaking any of the three creates real exposure.

Staffing is a different service entirely. A staffing agency provides people the business does not already employ. Under co-employment, the existing team stays exactly where it is, with only the administrative employer role shifting.

Nor does the arrangement erase worksite duties. Safety, lawful daily treatment of employees, and the accuracy of the information the business feeds into payroll all remain with the owner. A PEO cannot fix a schedule that was reported wrong.

Federal health coverage obligations deserve their own note. Applicable large employer status generally begins at 50 or more full-time employees including equivalents, and the Internal Revenue Service publishes the [employer rules under the Affordable Care Act](https://www.irs.gov/affordable-care-act/employers) that describe the determination. Broad context for how the rules affect smaller businesses is available through the federal [small business coverage overview](https://www.healthcare.gov/small-businesses/learn-more/how-aca-affects-businesses/). A vendor relationship does not move that determination off the business.

We are insurance nerds, not tax professionals. Payroll tax treatment inside a co-employment arrangement, credits, and any question about how the relationship affects a business return belong with a licensed tax professional.

## When does a PEO make sense, and when is it too much?

A PEO fits businesses that want large-group benefits pricing plus a full administrative offload, and interest typically starts somewhere around ten employees. Below that, a lighter model usually delivers more value per dollar.

Three questions separate the two situations.

**How much of the value is benefits?** A business whose main problem is losing candidates over health coverage is buying purchasing power, and that is what a PEO sells. A business whose main problem is that payroll takes four hours every other Friday is buying time, which costs far less to solve.

**How much administrative complexity actually exists?** Multiple worksites, multiple states, heavy turnover and workers compensation classifications all push toward a PEO. A stable ten-person office in one location does not generate that complexity.

**What happens at renewal?** PEO pricing is usually a percentage of payroll or a per-employee monthly fee, which means the cost scales with growth. The math is walked through in [how much does a PEO cost](/qa/how-much-does-a-peo-cost), and exit logistics matter before signing rather than after, which is covered in [can a business leave a PEO](/qa/can-a-business-leave-a-peo).
