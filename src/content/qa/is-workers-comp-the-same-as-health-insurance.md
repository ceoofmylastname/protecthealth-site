The confusion is understandable, because both systems pay hospital bills. A roofer who breaks a leg gets treatment paid either way. The question is what happens to the roofer who has a heart attack, and the answer is where the two systems stop resembling each other.

## What does workers comp actually cover?

Workers compensation covers injuries and illnesses that arise out of the job: the fall from the scaffold, the crush injury, the occupational disease traced to workplace exposure. Nothing that happens off the clock, and no one but the worker, is covered.

The system does three things health insurance does not. It pays medical costs for the work injury without deductibles or copays. It replaces a portion of lost wages while the worker cannot work. And it operates as a liability trade: the worker gets defined benefits without having to sue, and the employer gets protection from most injury lawsuits.

In Nevada, the system is regulated by the state's Division of Industrial Relations, and coverage requirements attach to employment. The [Nevada DIR](https://dir.nv.gov/) is the authority on who must be covered and how disputes get resolved, and construction has its own wrinkles around how subcontractors are treated that make assumptions dangerous in both directions. A 1099 contractor may not be covered by anyone's policy at all, which is worth verifying rather than discovering.

### What the certificate on file really means

Construction runs on certificates. A general contractor demands proof of workers comp before a sub sets foot on site, so subs carry the paperwork, and after enough years the paperwork starts to feel like insurance for the person carrying it.

It is not. The certificate documents that workers on that job are covered for injuries on that job. It says nothing about the sub's own status in some arrangements, nothing about anything that happens off-site, and precisely nothing about the sub's family, who were never on a job site in their lives.

## What does health insurance cover that workers comp never will?

Health insurance pays for medical care regardless of cause: illness, injuries away from work, preventive care, prescriptions, mental health care, and every covered family member's care. Cause is irrelevant, which is exactly the dimension where workers comp is strict.

Line the two up and the gap is stark. A cancer diagnosis, diabetes, a heart condition: health insurance territory, unless a rare occupational cause is proven. The Saturday ladder fall at home: health insurance. A child's ear infection, a spouse's surgery, the antibiotics for strep: health insurance, health insurance, health insurance. Workers comp has no opinion on any of it.

There is also the direction people forget. Health insurance generally does not pay for work injuries, because workers comp is the designated payer there. The systems are designed as adjacent tiles, not overlapping ones. A worker carrying only one tile is standing half on bare floor, and for most people the bare half is the bigger half, since bodies generate far more illness than job-site trauma over a lifetime.

The wage-replacement difference deserves its own sentence, because it cuts the opposite way. Workers comp pays a portion of lost wages while a work injury heals. Health insurance never replaces income at all: it pays medical bills and stops there. A self-employed tradesperson flattened for two months by an illness has no wage replacement from either system, which is a separate planning problem, and one more reason the two coverages cannot be mentally swapped for each other. They do different jobs even when they pay the same hospital.

### Why this hits construction workers hardest

Physical trades produce a double exposure. The work is hard on knees, backs, and shoulders, and the damage does not politely confine itself to work hours. The same body doing demolition on Tuesday is coaching Little League on Saturday, and only one of those days' injuries has a payer without health insurance. Meanwhile the industry's 1099 density means no employer is handing coverage over, a structural problem covered in full in the parent guide to [health insurance for construction contractors in Nevada](/blog-post/health-insurance-for-construction-contractors-nevada).

## What should a worker with only workers comp do?

Get actual health insurance, and use the marketplace to do it, because subsidies frequently make the real price far lower than the sticker price. For Nevadans without employer coverage, that means a plan through Nevada Health Link during open enrollment or after a qualifying life event.

The federal marketplace's guidance for [self-employed people](https://www.healthcare.gov/self-employed/) lays out the baseline: coverage is purchased individually, premium tax credits are based on estimated annual income, and nobody needs an employer to get covered. For trades with seasonal swings, the income estimate takes real thought, and the plan design question, what deductible a lumpy-income household can actually absorb, is its own decision, covered in [what coverage seasonal construction workers need](/qa/what-coverage-do-seasonal-construction-workers-need).

Two adjacent questions come up immediately in the trades. Whether a solo operator can get group-style pricing, answered honestly in [whether independent contractors can get group health rates](/qa/can-independent-contractors-get-group-health-rates). And whether the premiums are deductible, which for most self-employed people they generally are, with rules a tax professional should confirm, detailed in [whether health premiums are deductible for 1099 contractors](/qa/are-health-premiums-deductible-for-1099-contractors).

Covering the life takes one honest conversation, and it is worth having before the next season starts rather than after the injury that did not happen at work.
