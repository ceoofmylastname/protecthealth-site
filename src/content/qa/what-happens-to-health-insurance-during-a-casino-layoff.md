Las Vegas layoffs arrive in waves: after the holidays, during renovations, when a property changes hands, and hardest of all when the national economy catches a cold and tourism catches pneumonia. The paycheck stops immediately. What happens to health coverage is slower, and the slowness is where people get hurt.

## When does coverage actually end after a layoff?

It depends on which kind of coverage the worker had. Employer group plans typically run through the end of the month employment ends. Union hours-bank coverage continues until the banked hours are spent. Neither ends on the last shift, and the real end date is the date that matters.

For workers on an employer group plan, the plan document sets the termination date, and end-of-month is the common convention. A worker laid off on the 3rd may have most of a month of coverage left; a worker laid off on the 29th has days. HR or the plan administrator can confirm the date in one phone call, and that call is worth making before anything else.

Union workers at Strip properties often have more runway than they realize. Hours-bank designs bank hours worked above the monthly eligibility requirement, and the bank keeps fund coverage alive during a stretch without work. Depending on how full the bank was, that can mean weeks or months of continued coverage after a layoff. The fund office, not the employer, holds the balance and the rules, and calling for the exact figure beats guessing in either direction.

Every deadline that follows keys off the coverage end date, not the layoff date. Write it down.

## What are the three options once coverage ends?

Continuation coverage under COBRA, a marketplace plan through a special enrollment period, or Medicaid. The right choice depends on income during the gap, the doctors and treatments that cannot be interrupted, and how long the gap looks likely to last.

**COBRA continuation.** Federal law generally lets workers at covered employers keep the group plan after a layoff by paying the full premium themselves, plus an administrative charge of up to 2 percent. The Department of Labor's [continuation coverage overview](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/health-plans/cobra) covers the framework, including the election window. COBRA's virtue is sameness: identical plan, identical network, and the deductible already paid this year stays paid. Its vice is price, because the employer's share of the premium lands on the household at the worst possible moment.

**A marketplace special enrollment.** Losing coverage is a qualifying life event, and it opens a 60-day window to enroll through [Nevada Health Link](https://www.nevadahealthlink.com/). The household's new, lower income often qualifies for subsidies that make a marketplace plan dramatically cheaper than COBRA, and a mid-tier plan with financial help frequently beats paying full freight for the old plan. Income estimation matters here, and for tipped workers the estimate has its own traps, covered in [whether tip income counts toward health insurance subsidies](/qa/does-tip-income-count-toward-health-insurance-subsidies).

**Medicaid.** Nevada expanded Medicaid, so most adults qualify on income alone when household income falls below the expansion threshold. For a household with no income coming in, Medicaid is often the correct answer for the gap months: no premium, year-round enrollment, and switching to a marketplace plan later when work resumes is a normal, expected move.

## How should a laid-off worker choose among the three?

Run one comparison: total cost of each option for the months the gap is expected to last, measured against what care the household actually needs during those months.

A few patterns hold often enough to be useful. Short gap with active treatment underway favors COBRA, because continuity of doctors and an already-met deductible are worth paying for. Longer gap with routine needs favors the marketplace, because subsidies scale to the reduced income and the savings compound monthly. Income near zero favors Medicaid while it lasts.

One warning applies to all three: the 60-day special enrollment window does not pause while a household deliberates. Electing COBRA is allowed inside its own election period, and dropping COBRA later at will does not reopen the marketplace window, so sequencing matters. The safe order is to compare all three inside the first weeks, decide once, and enroll while every door is still open. A worker who does nothing for two months has one door left, and it is the emergency room.

Rehire timing also deserves a thought in this town, where layoffs are often seasonal and recall is common. Returning to work restarts employer or union eligibility on the plan's schedule, sometimes after a waiting period or a rebuilt hours bank, and a marketplace plan can be ended cleanly when the new coverage begins. Workers weighing whether returning union coverage beats keeping a marketplace plan should look at [whether union health coverage is better than marketplace coverage](/qa/is-union-health-coverage-better-than-marketplace-coverage), and workers recalled at reduced hours should look at [whether part-time hospitality workers can get marketplace coverage](/qa/can-part-time-hospitality-workers-get-marketplace-coverage).

## What does the layoff checklist look like?

Six items, in order, most of them phone calls.

Confirm the coverage end date with HR or the union fund office. Get the hours-bank balance in writing if a fund is involved. Collect the COBRA election notice and note its deadline. Build an honest income estimate for the months ahead, including unemployment benefits, which count as income for subsidy purposes. Compare COBRA, marketplace, and Medicaid against that estimate inside the first two weeks. Enroll before the 60-day window closes, whichever door wins.
