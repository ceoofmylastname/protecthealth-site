Rules of thumb answer the average household's question. DIME answers a specific household's question, and every input comes off a document that already exists.

## How does the DIME calculation work?

Four categories get totaled, then two categories get subtracted. The result is the shortfall a policy would need to cover.

1. **Debt.** Every non-mortgage balance. Credit cards, auto loans, personal loans, medical balances and private student loans, which frequently do not discharge at death the way federal loans generally do. Co-signed obligations belong here as well, because a co-signer remains liable.
2. **Income.** Annual earnings multiplied by the number of years a surviving household would need support. Ten years is a common default. The number of years until the youngest child is independent is a more honest horizon.
3. **Mortgage.** The payoff balance, not the monthly payment and not the purchase price.
4. **Education.** A realistic per-child figure for the schooling the household actually intends to fund, rather than a national average pulled off a chart.

Then subtract liquid savings and any life insurance already in force, including group coverage through an employer. The remainder is the gap. The worked version, with an illustrative Henderson household and a full table, sits in [the full coverage-math guide](/blog-post/how-much-life-insurance-do-i-need).

## Why does DIME beat multiplying income by a fixed number?

Because obligations diverge wildly between households earning identical money, and the published multiples disagree with each other by roughly a factor of two.

The [National Association of Insurance Commissioners consumer guidance on life insurance](https://content.naic.org/consumer/life-insurance.htm) notes that some insurance experts suggest five to eight times current income, then recommends working through a list of specific household questions to reach a more accurate amount. Other advisors routinely use ten to twelve times. Both ranges describe an average family, and the gap between them is enormous once applied to a real paycheck.

A renter with grown children and a homeowner with two toddlers can earn the same salary and carry completely different risk. DIME captures that difference in about fifteen minutes. The multiple still earns a role as a check on the result: if a DIME total lands at twenty times income, an input is probably wrong, and if it lands at two times, an obligation was probably missed.

## What does the DIME framework leave out?

Four things, and each one moves the number in a direction the acronym does not account for.

**Final expenses and estate settlement costs.** DIME has no letter for burial, cremation, probate or the administrative cost of closing an estate. Those arrive first and arrive fast.

**Childcare during the surviving parent's working years.** Education is in the framework. Daycare, after-school care and summer coverage are not, and in a two-earner household those costs land immediately rather than in eighteen years.

**Inflation.** A twenty year income replacement figure calculated in today's dollars is not a twenty year income replacement figure.

**Social Security survivor benefits.** These push the number down rather than up, which is why they get omitted more often than the others. The [Social Security Administration describes survivor benefits](https://www.ssa.gov/survivor) as monthly payments to eligible spouses, divorced spouses, children and dependent parents based on the deceased worker's earnings record. Amounts and eligibility are specific to that record, so the record deserves an actual look rather than an assumption in either direction.

## How does the DIME math shift for a Las Vegas household?

Two local realities change the shape of the total, and both push in the same direction.

The Mortgage line usually dominates. Valley housing costs have climbed enough that the payoff balance frequently outweighs every other category combined, which makes it the single most sensitive input in the calculation. Whether a death benefit should be aimed at that balance at all is worth reading in [does life insurance pay off a mortgage](/qa/does-life-insurance-pay-off-a-mortgage) before defaulting to yes.

The subtraction step often subtracts nothing. Clark County runs heavily on tipped hospitality work, gig platforms, commission roles and independent contracting, and none of that comes with employer life insurance. A household with two 1099 earners has no group policy to deduct, so the DIME gap is the whole obligation rather than the remainder. For households that do have workplace coverage, the amount usually disappoints once it sits next to the total, which is the point of [is employer life insurance enough](/qa/is-employer-life-insurance-enough).

One more local wrinkle on the Debt line. Nevada is a community property state, which affects how a deceased spouse's obligations can reach jointly held property. That is an attorney question rather than a broker question, and it is a good reason not to treat the Debt figure as theoretical.

## What should happen once the DIME number exists?

Two things, in order. The number gets pressure-tested, then it gets turned into a structure.

Pressure-testing means checking the inputs that carry the most weight: the mortgage payoff, the income horizon, and whether a non-earning partner was given a number at all. That last omission is the most common one in the framework, and it is addressed in [does a stay-at-home parent need life insurance](/qa/does-a-stay-at-home-parent-need-life-insurance).

Turning the number into a structure means deciding how long the coverage needs to last and whether any slice of the obligation is genuinely permanent. That decision comes after the arithmetic, never before, and the full comparison lives in [term versus whole life insurance](/blog-post/term-vs-whole-life-insurance). A DIME total is a specification. It is not a policy recommendation, and any conversation that jumps straight from the number to a named product without discussing duration has skipped a step worth being suspicious about.
