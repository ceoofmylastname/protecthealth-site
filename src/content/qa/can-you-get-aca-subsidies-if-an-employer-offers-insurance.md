The blocking mechanism is an offer, not a plan card in a wallet. That single distinction costs uninformed households real money every enrollment season.

## Why does an employer offer block ACA subsidies?

Because the law treats job-based coverage as the first line and marketplace credits as the backstop. An available offer that clears two federal standards removes the backstop.

Both tests have to be satisfied for the block to apply.

1. **Affordability.** The required employee contribution is compared against a percentage of household income that the IRS adjusts periodically. Contributions below that line make the offer affordable.
2. **Minimum value.** The plan has to cover a defined share of expected medical costs. A plan that fails this standard does not block the credit, no matter how cheap the contribution is.

Clear both and the premium tax credit is blocked, enrolled or not. Fail either and marketplace credits open back up, calculated exactly as described in [ACA premium tax credits explained](/blog-post/aca-premium-tax-credits-explained). The [IRS overview of the premium tax credit](https://www.irs.gov/affordable-care-act/individuals-and-families/the-premium-tax-credit-the-basics) sets out the federal definitions behind both tests.

Notice what is absent from that list. Plan quality is not a test. Network breadth is not a test. Whether the deductible feels reasonable is not a test. A plan that a household genuinely dislikes still blocks the credit if the contribution is affordable and the coverage clears minimum value.

## What happens when only part of a household is covered?

The analysis runs person by person rather than across the household as a single unit, and the answer for an employee is frequently different from the answer for a spouse or a child.

This is where Nevada households get tangled most often. One spouse works a W-2 job with an offer on the table. The other runs a real estate practice, a salon chair or a delivery route with no offer at all. The person holding the offer and the person without one do not automatically share the same outcome, and treating the household as a single yes or no answer is how money gets left behind.

The mechanics of who is tested against which cost figure have been revised by federal rulemaking, and the details matter enough that a household in this position should confirm the current plan year's treatment rather than working from a rule someone remembers from an earlier year. ## How does an ICHRA change the math?

An individual coverage HRA is not group coverage and does not follow the same rule. It creates a genuine choice, and choosing wrong is expensive.

Accepting an ICHRA forecloses the premium tax credit for that coverage outright. An ICHRA offer that fails the affordability test may instead be declined, with the household claiming the premium tax credit on a marketplace plan. One path or the other applies. Never both, and never a blend of the two.

That decision has to be run individual by individual, because a company-wide average hides the exact employee for whom the arrangement is a downgrade. An allowance that is generous for a young single employee in the southwest valley can sit well below the credit an older employee with a family would receive on the marketplace. The [federal guidance for employees offered an ICHRA](https://www.healthcare.gov/job-based-help/ichra/) walks through the employee side of the mechanics, the structure itself is explained in [what is an ICHRA](/blog-post/what-is-an-ichra), and the head-to-head comparison sits in [ICHRA versus marketplace health insurance](/blog-post/ichra-vs-marketplace-health-insurance).

For a Las Vegas household weighing a reimbursement offer against a subsidized plan already in hand, that comparison belongs in a conversation before anything gets signed. Reversing the decision later is not simple, and the enrollment calendar is unforgiving about second thoughts.

## Where do Nevada households get this wrong?

Four recurring errors, and each one shows up every winter.

**Declining an affordable offer and claiming a credit anyway.** The marketplace will let a household enroll and apply the credit based on what it entered. Reconciliation on the federal return is where the mistake surfaces, and by then twelve months of advance credit have to come back. What that settlement looks like is covered in [do ACA subsidies have to be paid back](/qa/do-aca-subsidies-have-to-be-paid-back).

**Assuming a weak plan unlocks credits.** Quality is not the test. A thin network and a high deductible are irritating, not disqualifying.

**Never re-checking after a renewal.** Employer contributions change every plan year, household income changes, and the affordability threshold is adjusted periodically. An offer that failed the test last year can pass this year, and the reverse happens just as often. Re-running the numbers each November is not busywork.

**Treating a spouse's situation as settled by the employee's.** Two people in one household can land on two different answers, and assuming otherwise is how a family overpays for a full year.

### One more layer for the coming plan year

Verification tightened alongside these rules. The automatic 60 day extension to resolve an income inconsistency was removed, leaving the 90 day statutory window standing on its own, and where the IRS holds no tax data for an applicant, self attestation of income is no longer accepted. Documents or another trusted data source are required instead. Both changes sit on the [2027 ACA changes page](/aca-changes), and both hit hardest in a household where one person recently left W-2 work.

Nevada has no state income tax, so the federal return is the only place any of this reconciles. There is no second filing where an error gets caught.
